§4 The L4 card, back side — the clamshell that explains 24 GB

This is the physical fact that most block diagrams hide, and it falls straight out of the probe. The bus is 192 bits, GDDR6 devices are 32-bit, so there are only 6 channels. But 6 channels × the 2 GB maximum density of a 16 Gb GDDR6 device is 12 GB — and the card has 24. The only resolution is two packages per channel, one on each face of the PCB, each running in ×16 mode.

Why NVIDIA did this. Clamshell is how you get datacenter capacity onto a single-slot, low-profile, 72 W card. The cost is that it buys capacity but not bandwidth — you still have only 6 channels and 192 bits, so 24 GB sits behind the same 300 GB/s. That ratio (12.5 GB of capacity per 100 GB/s of bandwidth) is unusually high, and it is exactly why the L4 pairs so well with inference on large models but poorly with bandwidth-bound HPC sweeps over all of memory.

§6 The AD104 die floorplan

The centrepiece. Three placement rules are physics, not preference, and they determine almost the whole layout: (1) anything that drives an off-die signal must sit on the die edge, because that is where the bump field can reach a package trace; (2) the L2 must sit between the GPCs and the memory controllers, because it is on the path from one to the other; (3) SMs come in physically adjacent pairs, because a TPC is a pair.

Why each block is where it is

BlockPositionPhysical reason it must be there
6 × GDDR6 PHYLeft, right and bottom edgesHard constraint. Each drives 32 data lines at 12.5 Gbps off-die. Those signals need the shortest possible path to the bump field and out to the package trace. A PHY in the die interior would need its high-speed signals routed across the whole chip first. Distributing them across three edges also spreads the heat and keeps trace lengths to the 12 packages matched.
PCIe Gen4 PHYBottom edge, centreHard constraint, same reason — 16 differential pairs at 16 GT/s. Bottom edge because the gold fingers are on the bottom edge of the card, so this is the shortest route to them.
L2, 6 × 8 MBCentral spine, in a bandTopological constraint. Every SM's miss must reach it and every DRAM access must pass through it. Central placement minimises the worst-case SM→L2 distance. It is split into 6 partitions so that each one sits near the memory controller it fronts, and so 6 lookups proceed in parallel rather than through one contended port.
CrossbarImmediately adjacent to L2It is the switch between the GPC ports and the L2 partitions. Every wire it carries is wide (hundreds of bits), so it must be short.
5 × GPCTwo rows, flanking the L2 spineThe GPCs are ~60% of the die area and have no off-die signals of their own, so they take the interior. Two rows either side of the L2 keeps every GPC roughly equidistant from it.
GigaThread EngineTop band, spanning all GPCsIt must fan block-dispatch wires out to all 58 SMs. A band spanning the GPC row minimises the longest dispatch path.
Media complexFills the corner left by 5 GPCs5 GPCs don't tile a rectangle evenly. The video engines are relatively latency-insensitive and self-contained, so they take the leftover corner — a real pattern on NVIDIA die shots.

Where the SRAM actually is, spatially. Adding up §6–§9: 48 MB of L2 in the central spine, 7.4 MB of L1/shared distributed as 58 separate 128 KB blocks inside the SMs, and 14.5 MB of register file distributed as 232 separate 64 KB blocks inside the sub-partitions. That is ~70 MB of SRAM, and only the L2 third of it is in one place. The other two thirds are deliberately scattered right next to the lanes that consume them — which is the whole architectural argument for shared memory over cache: it is not faster because it is a different technology, it is faster because it is physically closer and needs no tag lookup or coherence.

Two placements here carry real meaning.

1 · The 128 KB SRAM is one array, not three. What the areas tell you that the spec sheet doesn't.
· The register file is the biggest block — bigger than the entire FP32 lane array it feeds. Registers are multi-ported SRAM, and ports cost area quadratically. This is the physical reason register pressure is a real constraint: 65,536 registers per SM is not an arbitrary limit, it is what fits.
· The tensor core rivals the register file for area while the 32 FP32 lanes are a thin strip. Drawn to scale, this is the story of the last three GPU generations.
· The warp scheduler is small, and there is no reorder buffer, no branch predictor, no register renaming anywhere on this floorplan. All that area went into lanes and SRAM instead. That is the bargain: the GPU spends nothing on extracting instruction-level parallelism, and in exchange you must supply thread-level parallelism — 12 warps per scheduler — or the lanes sit idle.

This is why the L2 is banked and the crossbar is pipelined. A wire on a global metal layer crossing the 18.6 mm width of this die cannot deliver a signal in one 490 ps clock cycle — not because of the speed of light, but because RC delay on a thin copper wire forces you to break it into repeated segments, each costing a stage. So a long on-die path is not one slow wire, it is a pipeline. Every millimetre a request travels toward the L2 spine is latency you pay in pipeline stages and arbitration, and that is the real content of the “~250–350 cycle” L2 latency.

§12 Distance vs. latency — the surprise

Now that everything is placed, we can measure the actual copper distance of each hop and compare it to the observed latency. The result is counter-intuitive and worth internalising: at every single level, the speed of light is essentially free.

Signal velocity is about 7 ps/mm both on a buffered on-die global wire and on an FR-4 PCB trace (≈0.47 c in both cases, for different reasons). One GPU clock at 2.04 GHz is 490 ps. So:

So the floorplan does not explain the latency numbers. Every hop is ~1000× slower than the distance requires. Latency on this machine is made almost entirely of protocol, arbitration, queueing and pipeline depth — not geometry. If you have been carrying a mental model where "L2 is slow because it's far away", replace it: L2 is slow because a request has to lose an L1 tag check, win a crossbar arbitration, traverse repeater stages, win a bank arbitration, and wait behind 57 other SMs doing the same thing.

Which raises the obvious question: if distance is free, why does physical layout matter at all? §13.

§13 Distance vs. energy — why the floorplan matters after all

Distance does not cost you time. It costs you joules — because moving a bit means charging the capacitance of a wire, and capacitance scales with length. On a card with a probed 72.00 W ceiling, joules are the binding constraint.

Read GDDR6~30 mm, off-die~225 pJ (~7 pJ/bit)~150×
Move over PCIe to host~300 mm, off-board~300+ pJ~200×

What that does to the 72 W budget — arithmetic from probed numbers

Peak FP32 arithmetic:
  30.29 TFLOPS ÷ 2 flop/FMA = 15.15e12 FMA/s  ×  1.5 pJ   =  22.7 W

Peak DRAM traffic:
  300 GB/s × 8 bit/B = 2.4e12 bit/s          ×  7 pJ/bit  =  16.8 W

  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Both at once                                             =  39.5 W
  Plus: leakage, the clock tree across 294 mm², L2 + register file
        accesses feeding all of it, PHY static power, VRM loss
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Board budget PROBED                                       =  72.00 W
This closes the loop between the floorplan and the performance model. Saturating both the FP32 lanes and the memory bus would consume over half the board's entire power budget on those two activities alone, before any of the overheads. The card physically cannot do it — so it clocks down. This is why:
  • A dense kernel on an L4 will not sustain 2,040 MHz, and you must measure sustained rather than burst throughput.
  • Keeping data on-chip is an energy optimisation first and a latency optimisation second. Every DRAM read you convert into a shared-memory read saves ~150× the energy — which the power manager returns to you as clock frequency.
  • Ada's 48 MB L2 (§6) is not just a latency feature. At ~40 pJ versus ~225 pJ, an L2 hit costs about 1/6th the energy of a DRAM access. On a 72 W passively-cooled card, that cache is a power-efficiency device, and it is the single biggest reason an L4 delivers what it does inside its envelope.
